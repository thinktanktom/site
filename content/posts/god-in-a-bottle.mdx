Sometimes I wish I had been born in the hunter-gatherer epoch. Oh, what I wouldn't give to traipse around the Serengeti in my ethically sourced leopard skin, with my trusty <Annotated n={1}>spear</Annotated>, poking the occasional giraffe and spearing dates and figs.

My only worry would be the setting of the sun and my fictional wife's despair at yet another dinner of dates and figs.

Unfortunately, I live in the age of the Internet and that of AI. Last week I got sick of not knowing enough about the internals of the AI systems that I’m using and building, and went on a bit of a knowledge bender. Now, I’m aware that a week isn’t a lot of time to comb through every nook and cranny of machine learning. I wasn’t aiming to be an ML researcher. I just wanted to understand what was happening under the hood, so that I could more effectively control the agentic systems and workflows I build. For the sake of scope, this post will discuss <Annotated n={2}>genAI, LLMs</Annotated> and agents, but some, if not all, of these concepts do apply across the broad field of machine learning. So without further ado, and with a deep sigh, let's get into it.

## What goes into building an AI?

Everything. No, I'm serious. Scrape the entirety of the internet, throw most of it out as junk, add books, code and licensed data, and distill what's left into <Annotated n={3}>tokens</Annotated>. Then train the machine on a near-infinite "guess the next token" gauntlet. What you get is a system that outputs probability distributions with respect to what token is most likely to come next.

It's like an RPG character with billions of stats, except none of them have names. There's no "Strength" or "Intelligence," just billions of unlabelled sliders that only make sense all together. We get the machine to nudge each slider slightly every time the model guesses wrong. Very similar to what we'd do if our best friend asked us to DJ at his wedding on a day's notice.

As you might've guessed, different use cases have different perspectives on what is important. This is why the dataset you curate isn't trivial. Once the model is trained, you can fine-tune it on a smaller, curated, domain-specific dataset to more aptly fit your case.

After all this hubbub, you get something called a base model. Like many of my connections on LinkedIn, it will blatantly lie about knowing something it doesn't know and, when asked a question, blurts out prose without a single generative thought.

That's where post-training comes in. We get human labellers to answer thousands of questions and <Annotated n={4}>rank</Annotated> the model's responses. Manually. This further improves the probability of a more polished answer and a better experience overall.

## Is an AI capable of thinking?

As much as I'd love for everyone to get along, in reality it is convention for most frontier labs to keep the messy details of their post-training process from the public. However, DeepSeek, a Chinese AI lab, published a paper in January 2025, detailing the benefits it saw from reinforcement learning. Its headline finding was that strong reasoning emerged from reinforcement learning with very little human-written example data. One variant, R1-Zero, had none at all.

Reinforcement learning is not a new concept. It goes back decades, and AlphaGo is its most famous party trick. In one powerful version of it, we give the AI a problem whose answer we already know and then watch it figure out different ways to hack at it. Kind of like handing your child a really tight jar of Nutella and leaving the room. Over time, the model had a series of what is described as 'aha' moments, where it spontaneously learns to stop, double-check its work and backtrack when an approach isn't working.

Keep in mind that under the hood we are still dealing with probability distributions. What has changed is that reasoning models now write out a long chain of intermediate "thinking" tokens before giving a final answer. Each new token builds on everything before it, so the model is effectively doing scratch work it can then build upon. This is often called test-time compute: spending more computation while answering, rather than during training. This is less thinking and more what I'd call active computation. Although I can't help but notice how eerily similar it all is to the way us humans learn things.

Nowadays, everyone is using reinforcement learning in their post training effort. The 'deep think' or 'thinking' modes you see in most chatbots are where this training shows up.

## What is prompting?

Out of the box, AI is a jack of all trades that knows nothing about you: your situation, your constraints or your goals. Adding a list of words or a direction allows us to better steer the probability distribution of the output. It's an emulation of the preference training done by human labellers mentioned earlier.

A system prompt sets standing instructions for the whole conversation. Adding a <Annotated n={5}>few worked examples in the prompt</Annotated> can show the model exactly what you want. This becomes much more useful when creating agents and building agentic workflows.

## What are agents? What about agentic workflows?

Remember Agent Smith from The Matrix? I always thought his ability to clone himself into whichever role he needed was way cooler than whatever Neo had going on.

When someone mentions agents, it always reminds me of the image of little test-tube Agent Smiths trapped in small glass boxes. They are compute-wise the same as your average LLM, except they are given a narrative or a mission via prompting, along with a set of tools to carry it out. I think of them as goombahs for a big mafia family. You give them a hit, they have to decide how to go about it, what tool to use and bring back proof that the job is done. Without all that, it's just an <Annotated n={6}>LLM with a fancy prompt</Annotated>.

As for agentic workflows, the difference is who holds the wheel. In a workflow, your code decides the sequence of LLM calls and tools along a predefined path. In an agent, the model decides its own next step. I'm not going to go into too much detail on creating agentic workflows and the like, as everyone does it differently with different skills. I would suggest you try to build your own whilst taking inspiration from good engineers out there, as simply <Annotated n={7}>copying</Annotated> takes the fun out of it.

## Conclusion

AI is kind of like our version of the pyramids. The amount of work put into this endeavour is immense and mandates a <Annotated n={8}>collective vision</Annotated> at scale. My personal view on AI is a little nuanced. Over time, it's been interesting to watch math and computer science become increasingly esoteric in nature. Even the people building these models can't fully explain why a trained model works the way it does.

Reading through the research, I found the whole process and big-picture output reminiscent of my brief foray into alchemy. One of the frontiers of alchemy is research into the fabled 'Homunculus', or the artificial human. I think that's exactly what these companies are trying to build: God in a bottle. Omnipotent and readily usable. I suspect the stochasticity of our universe will prevent us from ever achieving this goal, but the question isn't whether we'd succeed or fail. It's how close is close enough?
