The Proud Robot.

aiwritingmeta

When OpenAI first released ChatGPT, I scarcely used it. It was not until late 2024 that I worked it into my workflow in earnest, and by then I had learned its limits: if the model failed to find a fix within the first two or three prompts, I was in for a wild goose chase down some obscure rabbit hole. In those cases, the trusted trinity of Stack Overflow, GitHub Issues and the odd tech forum served me far better.

Cut to building this website and I wanted to see how far I could stretch it. Although I advertise myself as a "full-stack developer" for the sake of the layman, those familiar with my work know that while I can build a website, I rarely enjoy it. The JavaScript, HTML and CSS boilerplate¹ (see note 1) I once wrote by hand still gives me nightmares.

So, after a couple of YouTube videos² (see note 2) and some documentation, I built an agentic workflow³ (see note 3). I supplied the land, the water and the seeds, while my army of farmers, all named Claude, handled the tilling and the planting.

No more Google searches for obscure CSS spacing issues or half-forgotten syntax, and no more giving up halfway after a week of wading through documentation. Simply architect it, build it, deploy it, test it and audit it. Something about the whole process stuck with me, like a burnt-bread connoisseur who had just discovered the toaster. It gave me my fix of building while cutting out much of the brain strain that usually comes with it.

With the website reasonably finished, there remained the small matter of content. Still riding that high, I added a note-taking feature to the workflow and began spinning up technical documentation on the fly. I never bothered to read most of it. It existed only as a record of what I had done, in case I ever needed to consult it.

That day came sooner than expected. One of those posts covered an agentic server box⁴ (see note 4) I had built for a client, and some time after I had wired it up for him, he wanted another. I went back to the documentation and found that I could only vaguely remember the work. I recalled some of the bugs and the difficulty, but scarcely how I had fixed any of it. Worse, the prose itself irritated me in a way I could not put my finger on at the time. It made me cringe.

Rewriting those posts was something I wanted to do yet never got around to. It was not until I visited LinkedIn, that mecca of AI-generated content, that the cause finally hit me. Somewhere between the ninth and fifteenth post, I found I could intuitively tell AI-generated spiel from authentic writing, and I recognised the same spiel in my own posts. Even the comments were AI-generated yes-men, built to people-please and kiss whichever ring was nearest. The realisation that I had become one of these poor folks, who outsourced not just their voices but their very opinions to a communal cesspit of borrowed thought, sickened me.

It all reminded me of the "Proud Robot"⁵ (see note 5). A short story I read as a child about a drunk who built an obscenely advanced and vain robot but could not remember why⁶ (see note 6). The punchline of the story was that the robot ended up being built for the sole purpose of opening cans.

Similarly drunk on the thrill of building and accomplishing things, I awoke to find that my own obscenely advanced machine had produced a flavourless tech blog about a product I barely remembered building.

None of this is a grievance against AI itself. I think it is great within its box, which for now is a human-in-the-loop use case. With the arrival of Jev and the System One models likely to follow it, we may yet see genuine automation of menial tasks.

For my part, I have decided to be more mindful of what I put out there. A good craftsman never blames his tools, nor does he ask them to make his decisions for him.
